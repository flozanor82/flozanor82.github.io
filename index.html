Felipe A. Lozano Rojas, Ph.D.

Associate Professor Public Budgeting and Finance
School of Public and International Affairs - University of Georgia
Email: flozano@uga.edu

My research interests lie in the intersection between public finance and social policy. I focus on taxation and behavior and on revenue-generating social-policies that provide different levels of governments with fiscal independence while at the same time tackling social problems, and substance and health issues, in particular. My specific interests include consumption taxation effects on local government finances, excise taxation to influence behavior, the U.S. opioid epidemic, consequences of the COVID-19 pandemic, and, more recently, changes to the SNAP program.

As examples of this intersection, my article in Health Economics shows that Philadelphia's soda tax reduced sugar purchases well beyond the beverages it directly taxed — evidence that excise taxes can shape health-relevant consumption more broadly than their statutory base suggests. On the local-finance side, my article in Public Budgeting & Finance uses the DEA's 2014 hydrocodone rescheduling — an exogenous shock that pushed some users toward heroin and drove up opioid mortality — to trace how county governments responded fiscally: health and public-welfare expenditures rose consistently in exposed communities, while policing and corrections spending was unchanged. The added funding came largely from intergovernmental transfers that had already begun climbing as the epidemic unfolded, consistent with SAMHSA and other federal grants flowing to local governments.

This agenda has recently grown along both fronts. On the behavioral/health side, in an article conditionally accepted at the American Economic Journal: Economic Policy, together with Prof. David Bradford, we trace a 2007 surge of diverted prescription opioids to Colombian drug-trafficking organizations and show it reduced overall opioid mortality by shifting the illicit market toward more predictable-potency drugs. In a forthcoming American Journal of Health Economics article, with Prof. Amanda Abraham, Prof. Sumedha Gupta and Prof. David Bradford, we use a synthetic-control approach to show that medical cannabis laws reduced opioid prescribing among commercially insured patients by nearly 16%. On the public-finance side, a 2026 article in the International Review of Economics and Finance, with Prof. Luis Navarro (University of Washington), shows that the Federal Reserve's Municipal Liquidity Facility and other early pandemic interventions substantially reduced volatility in municipal bond markets, particularly for higher-rated state and local issuers. I am also extending my consumption-tax agenda to food assistance: with Prof. David Frisvold, I am studying how SNAP purchase-restriction waivers affect beverage purchases — SNAP served an average of 41.7 million Americans a month (12.3% of the population) at a federal cost of over $100 billion in the most recent fiscal year, so even modest behavioral responses carry real fiscal weight.

In addition to the previously mentioned, my research has been published in journals such as Health Affairs, the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, Demography, the Municipal Finance Journal, JAMA Health Forum and JAMA Network Open, and my work has been portrayed by media outlets such as US News & World Report, the Wall Street Journal, Tax Foundation, and NPR. I joined the University of Georgia School of Public and International Affairs, as an Assistant Professor of Public Budgeting and Finance in the Fall of 2020, and was promoted to Associate Professor in 2026. I received my PhD in Public Affairs from Indiana University's O'Neill School in the Spring of 2020, holding concentrations in Public Finance and Policy Analysis and a minor in Data Science from the School of Informatics. Previously, I served as a Consultant for the World Bank, in matters related to student financial aid, and I was the Chief Planning Officer (2014) for ICETEX, the student loan agency in my home country, Colombia.
